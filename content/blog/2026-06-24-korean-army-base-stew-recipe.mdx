If you've watched Korean dramas set in Seoul or binge-watched street food content, you've probably seen a bubbling red stew filled with processed meat, vegetables, and noodles, shared from a single pot. That's budae jjigae—army base stew—one of Korea's most beloved comfort foods with a fascinating backstory.

Budae jjigae wasn't born in a royal kitchen or a five-star restaurant. After the Korean War, American military bases near Seoul left behind surplus canned goods: Spam, hot dogs, beans. Resourceful Korean cooks transformed these ingredients into something uniquely their own by adding gochugaru (red chili flakes), garlic, and ramen noodles. What started as a way to make do became a cultural icon. Today, it's less about scarcity and more about joy—the comfort of sharing a sizzling, tangy, slightly spicy stew with friends.

You'll find budae jjigae at pojangmacha (street tent restaurants) and dedicated chains across Korea, especially near universities and nightlife districts. Koreans eat it year-round, though it's especially popular as a late-night meal or casual dinner among groups. The ritual is important: everyone gets their own spoon, you cook and eat from the same pot, and you bond while the steam rises.

## What you need

The beauty of budae jjigae is flexibility. Here are the essentials, but feel free to swap based on what you find:

- A can of Spam (or ham, hot dogs—the processed meat is the identity)
- A handful of sliced Korean fish cakes (eomuk)
- A few slices of American cheese (yes, really—it melts and adds creaminess)
- One pack of instant ramen noodles (don't cook it first)
- A handful of baked beans or kidney beans
- Half an onion, sliced
- A small handful of mushrooms, sliced
- Two or three garlic cloves, minced
- A splash of gochugaru (Korean red chili flakes)
- A splash of gochujang (Korean red chili paste)
- One tablespoon of soy sauce
- A splash of sesame oil
- About 4 cups of water or light broth
- Salt and pepper to taste
- Optional: green onion for garnish, a splash of kimchi juice for tanginess

## The five-step method

**Step one: Build your base broth.** Heat a tablespoon of sesame oil in a large pot or shallow cast iron skillet (the kind with sides works best). Add your minced garlic and let it toast for about a minute until fragrant. Pour in your water or broth. Add a generous pinch of gochugaru, a heaping spoon of gochujang, and the soy sauce. Stir until it's smooth and the broth turns a rich red color. This is your flavor foundation—taste it and adjust the spice and saltiness now.

**Step two: Prepare your protein and vegetables.** Slice your Spam into thin rectangles (or cut hot dogs into bite-sized pieces). Arrange your Spam, fish cakes, onion slices, and mushrooms around the pot in a visually pleasing way. There's an aesthetic to budae jjigae—it should look abundant and inviting.

**Step three: Layer and simmer.** Bring the broth to a boil, then add your beans and let everything simmer for about five minutes. The vegetables will start to soften and the Spam will begin to brown at the edges (this is where the flavor magic happens).

**Step four: Add the cheese and noodles.** Tear up the American cheese slices and drop them into the pot. Add your uncooked ramen noodles directly into the bubbling stew. They'll soften and absorb the spicy broth. Stir gently so everything is submerged. Let this cook for about three to four minutes until the noodles are tender but still have a slight bite.

**Step five: Finish and serve.** Add a splash of sesame oil, a sprinkle of green onion if you have it, and a crack of pepper. Some people add a squirt of kimchi juice at the end for extra tang—trust this instinct. Taste one more time and adjust seasoning. The stew should be spicy, savory, slightly sweet from the beans, and creamy from that melted cheese.

## A note on the ritual

Budae jjigae is a sharing dish. Traditionally it arrives at your table still bubbling, and you eat straight from the pot with your friends or family, each person claiming a spoon's worth. It's not formal; it's intimate and chaotic in the best way. If you're cooking this at home, serve it in a shallow pot or skillet set over a tabletop burner or hot plate to keep it bubbling throughout the meal.

The flavors will deepen as it sits and simmers. Don't rush. Let people tear off pieces of Spam, fish cakes, and noodles as they wish. Add more broth if it reduces too much. This is the opposite of a fussy, plated dish—it's alive and evolving.

Budae jjigae tells a story of resilience, creativity, and the Korean gift for turning anything into something delicious. When you eat it, you're tasting history and community at once. And now you can make it anytime.
